Oksana's is a counter at the foot of Pylon Forty, cut into the concrete where a maintenance bay used to be, open to the street under the Spine's deck behind a roll-up steel shutter. Six stools, a zinc counter worn bright where elbows go, a steel urn of black tea that is never empty, tea served in glasses in tin holders, and one dish worth crossing the Spine for: dumplings, boiled or fried, with whatever filling was cheap, sour cream if you ask, dill whether you ask or not. An old radio plays the freight report and talk shows nobody listens to. When a heavy hauler crosses the joint overhead, the glasses walk an inch along the counter, and the regulars put out a hand without looking. Oksana runs it alone. She feeds the night-shift lift crews before they start, keeps a tab for the hangers, and never asks anyone what they do. Spiners say nothing said at her counter reaches the street, and that she knows who's missing before their families do.
